Lifecycle benchmark: VizDOM vs end-to-end grounders

One step set, one host, one scoring. VizDOM parses each screen once into a Visual DOM and resolves every test step against it, by exact text (text=) or by description through its own language tier (desc=). Three end-to-end grounders, ELAM-7B, UI-TARS-1.5-7B and Aria-UI, run a vision-language model on every step instead. All of them perform the same 514 steps on the same 44 screens and are scored on the same three outcomes: did the click land, was a true statement confirmed, and was an absent target rejected.

One host, one exception. Every column ran on the RTX 6000 Ada host described in section 2, except Aria-UI: a 25 B mixture-of-experts model with about 50 GB of bf16 weights, which does not fit a 48 GB card and ran on a 96 GB card with the same steps, scoring and adapter code. Its accuracy is comparable with the other columns; its latency is not, and its row is marked wherever it appears.

VizDOM's accuracy columns are deterministic. On fixed software versions they reproduce step for step on every card they were run on, so they depend on the software and not on the GPU. They are quoted with the OCR stage complete: easyocr plus the Tesseract re-read of low-confidence boxes (ADR-012).

1. Summary

Clicking. With its language tier, VizDOM lands 204 of 213 clicks (0.958): within 0.4 points of Aria-UI (0.962) and above UI-TARS-1.5-7B (0.944) and ELAM-7B (0.930). With exact text= locators it lands 195 (0.916) with the OmniParser or the modular detector and 181 (0.850) with UIED. Almost every VizDOM miss is a resolution miss: a caption OCR did not read, or a locator that matches more than one element, which the exactly-one contract refuses to click.

Judging. Asked whether a control that is not on the screen is visible, Aria-UI answers PASSED on 88 of 88, ELAM on 86 and UI-TARS on 60. VizDOM answers FAILED on all 88 in every configuration, because a statement needs exactly one matching element in the DOM and an absent label has none. VizDOM's own verify errors, 8 with desc= and 13 with text=, are all false fails on captions OCR could not read: the test stops and a person looks. The best grounder at clicking is the worst oracle.

Cost. VizDOM pays per screen: load once, parse each of the 44 states once, then answer every step from the cached DOM. A grounder pays per step: 514 model inferences. On this host that is 0.04 to 0.10 s per step for VizDOM against 2.72 s for UI-TARS and 4.00 s for ELAM (Aria-UI 1.57 s on its faster card), so 15 to 100 times less time per step, and the test runner itself needs no GPU.

Reading for the thesis. A GUI test oracle must not pass a check whose target is absent. That is the one failure class this benchmark isolates with its 88 negative statements, and it is where the architecture difference shows: 0 for a resolved-DOM system against 0.20 to 0.29 for per-step vision-language grounding, at a small fraction of the inference cost. With the language tier in front of a deterministic DOM, VizDOM gives up 0.4 points of click accuracy to the best grounder and gains a correct verdict on every absent target.

4. Clicking: within half a point of the best grounder

Action-hit rate per executor

Share of the 213 click steps whose produced point fell inside the target element. ▲ Longer bar is better; 1.00 means every click landed.

Aria-UI lands 205 of 213, VizDOM with its language tier 204, UI-TARS 201 and ELAM 198. Exact text= locators land 195 with the OmniParser or the modular detector, which are step-identical, and 181 with UIED. The language tier gains its nine extra clicks on captions that exact text cannot match but token overlap plus the role word can: the placeholder Search..., which OCR reads without its dots, and path-like values such as APAC\USER01.

Where VizDOM misses

  • Almost every miss is a resolution miss. The locator finds no element or more than one, and the exactly-one contract refuses to click rather than guess; a click on a wrong element is the rare exception.
  • With text= (18 misses for OmniParser and modular): eight Search... placeholders, four data-table digits whose locator matches 12 to 18 cells of the page, three APAC\USER01 values and three others. UIED adds its own fragmentation, steps where it yields two boxes for one control (32 misses).
  • With desc= (9 misses): repeated table digits, where the margin between candidates is zero, and captions OCR still misreads. That is the floor of this design: what OCR cannot read and what a description cannot single out.

Where the grounders miss

  • ELAM (15 misses) fails mostly in the data-table family: 8 of its 28 click steps there, the small numbered pagination and row controls. The rest are four settings rows, two value fields and one login control.
  • UI-TARS (12 misses) fails on the login screens: 5 of 12 steps, including three social-login buttons; its login hit rate is 0.58 against ELAM's 0.92. The other seven are four value fields and three settings rows.
  • Aria-UI (8 misses) is perfect on dialog, info_panel, login and settings. Its misses are five form_sidebar input fields, one data-table control and two toolbar buttons. Every miss produced a point; it was outside the target box.

5. Judging: the oracle row

Outcome of the 301 verify steps per executor

Each bar sums to 301. ▲ A longer blue segment is better; the green "false pass" segment is the one to minimise, and 0 (no green at all) is the only acceptable value for a test oracle. Orange false fails are cheap: the test stops and a person looks.

correct verdictfalse fail (true statement, said FAILED)false pass (absent target, said PASSED)

The negative statements use labels that cannot exist on a business screen, in the form "the 'Frobnicate' button is visible", expected verdict FAILED.

  • ELAM uses its native verdict mode: the model-card prompt asks it to reason step by step and conclude PASSED or FAILED. It concluded PASSED on 86 of the 88 impossible statements. Reading its outputs, it maps the unknown label onto a nearby real element and then confirms that element is visible. It also produced three false fails on true statements.
  • UI-TARS has no verdict mode; the verdict is whether it grounds the subject to a point. It grounded 60 of the 88 nonsense labels to some element and never refused a true statement. Its refusals are label-dependent (it rejected Frobnicate 13 times of 17), not random.
  • Aria-UI uses the same proxy and returned a point for every one of the 301 statements, all 88 impossible ones included: 0.292 is the ceiling of this metric. The model that grounds best is the one that never declines to ground.
  • VizDOM resolves the statement against the parsed DOM. An absent label has zero matches, so the verdict is FAILED by construction, with text= and with desc=, where the tier chain declines all 88 because no candidate scores high enough and the text model, shown a short candidate table, answers none. Its errors are false fails on captions OCR could not read: 13 with text=, 8 with desc=.

The ratio depends on how many steps a suite runs against each screen. Here it is 514 steps over 44 states, about 12 steps per state: VizDOM with OmniParser is 15 times cheaper per step than Aria-UI, 26 times cheaper than UI-TARS and 39 times cheaper than ELAM; the modular backend is 26, 44 and 65 times cheaper. 8. The language tier (desc=)

The text= columns compare VizDOM without its language tier against grounders that are all language. The desc= column closes that gap: every step also carries a description derived from its caption ("Save button", "Remember me checkbox", "Warp Cores button" for the negatives), resolved through the tier chain of ADR-022 over the same parse-once DOM. The production chain scores the description lexically against the DOM first and asks the 3B text model only when the scoring is not confident, over a short candidate table with null as an allowed answer. The model tiers were also measured on their own, to show what the order buys.

The two vision rows were measured on this host before the Tesseract re-read was installed, so their lexical tier answers 398 steps instead of 408. Their verdict on the vision model does not depend on it.

  • The lexical tier does the work. 408 of 514 steps are answered with no model call, in a fraction of a millisecond each. The text model is asked about the other 106, the 88 negatives plus the positives with no confident lexical winner, and answers 2. The chain gains nine clicks over text= and declines all 88 absent targets.
  • The text model alone is a poor grounder. Over the whole DOM table (30 to 110 rows) it resolves fewer steps and picks confidently wrong, unrelated elements on most of its misses ("Prev button" → a static text John Doe), and it rationalises 5 of the 88 nonsense captions onto real controls ("Warp Cores button" → the Time button, "Frobnicate button" → a Save button, at confidence 0.90 to 1.00). The same model over a short candidate list declines all of them.
  • The 3B vision model is not usable as a tier. On a fixed block of screens it loops on a repeated token until the model server aborts the call (394 of 514 steps alone, 80 of 116 behind the shortlist); where it does answer, it behaves like the grounders and passes 17 of the 30 absent targets it was asked. It stays out of the production chain.

Reading for the thesis. The ADR-022 design, deterministic scoring first and a small model only where the scoring is not confident, is what makes VizDOM's language tier an oracle: the model sees a short list with null as an allowed answer, and its answer is validated against the DOM. The tier order matters as much as the model. The caveat is the phrasing: the descriptions here are caption plus role word, the lexical tier's ideal input, so 0.958 is an upper bound for this phrasing; a tester's own wording exercises the model tier more.

10. Threats to validity

  • Synthetic screens. All 44 states come from the project's generator. Real applications have denser, noisier layouts; absolute scores will differ there. A real-world labelled set is the next measurement.
  • Generated descriptions. Caption plus role word is exactly what the lexical tier scores, so the desc= figures are an upper bound for that phrasing.
  • Easy negatives. A nonsense label can never partially match a DOM, which favours VizDOM; a model that reads the screen might be expected to reject "Zylophone", and mostly did not. Plausible-but-absent labels ("Export" on a screen with "Import") would be the harder test, for VizDOM's OCR as much as for the grounders.
  • Proxy verdicts. UI-TARS and Aria-UI have no verdict mode, so "grounded a point" counts as PASSED. ELAM's native verdict is the fairer comparison, and it still passes 86 of 88 absent targets.
  • Grounder variation across cards. Greedy bf16 decoding moved the grounders by a few steps between GPU generations, about one point; on the same card model they reproduced exactly. The VizDOM columns reproduced exactly everywhere.
  • VizDOM's timings are from this host's OCR configuration without the Tesseract re-read, which runs only on boxes easyocr is not confident about and adds parse time; per-step cost stays an order of magnitude below the grounders either way.
  • Aria-UI ran on a different card (section 2.1). Its accuracy is comparable; its latency is not, and its remote code ran without grouped_gemm.
